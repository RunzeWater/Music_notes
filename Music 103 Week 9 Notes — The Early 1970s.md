## Singer-songwriters (Week 9.1)

Singer-songwriters wrote intensely personal songs (intimate, introspective, confessional), applying lyric poetry to semi-autobiographical themes. The style, dubbed "soft rock", was massively successful and is the antithesis of hard rock and heavy metal.

### From Woodstock to the turn inward (Oct 26 lecture)

- **Woodstock (1969)** and **Monterey Pop** became famous documentaries. Writers at the time noted the oddity of a counterculture packaged and sold for the price of a theatre ticket. Woodstock is remembered as the literal and symbolic end of the 1960s.
- These festivals had very few women and few Black performers; Janis Joplin and Jimi Hendrix were outliers.
- **Altamont** (part of a Rolling Stones tour): moved to a smaller site, Hells Angels hired as security, violence and a death, the Stones helicoptered out. It exposed the gap between rock stars and fans actually fighting in the streets.
- A new generational divide: high-school-aged listeners moved toward stripped-down, riff-based hard rock; people who came up through the 1960s (now entering their 30s) moved toward soft rock.
- Some saw the genre as selling out the counterculture by making peace with family life and home.

### What defines the genre

- **A turn inward**: a retreat from, or a re-evaluation of, 1960s politics? Lyrics drew on performers' own divorces, hardships and parenthood, blurring public and private.
- **"The personal is political"** (feminist maxim). A high number of women in the genre meant more songs told from women's perspectives.
- Roots: 1960s reflexivity (the Beatles) and the folk revival, which was fading. Woody Guthrie (poetic individualism), Bob Dylan (autobiography), Joan Baez (personal sincerity), Simon & Garfunkel (college audiences).
- Some performers came out of the **Brill Building** songwriting world (Carole King).
- Image: black-and-white photos, direct eye contact, an acoustic guitar, the inside of the artist's home (Carly Simon, James Taylor). Compare the costumes and absent faces of *Sgt. Pepper* or the White Album.
- Sound: built on **accompaniment patterns, not riffs**. The **rhythmic arpeggio** (chord notes played in sequence) via **fingerpicking** feels warm and inviting, not a wall of distorted noise. Not universal: "Big Yellow Taxi" uses strummed chords.
- James Taylor examples in class: "You've Got a Friend" (written by Carole King) and "Fire and Rain".

### Carole King and *Tapestry*

- Born 1942, New York City; a Brill Building professional who wrote for girl groups.
- After her divorce from Gerry Goffin she moves to the West Coast and teams with producer Lou Adler.
- *Writer* (1970) struggled; ***Tapestry* (1971)** broke all existing sales records. In 1971 she outsold everyone in the music business, just after the Beatles broke up.
- Neither consumed by politics nor driven to share intimate details of her private life. She projected contentment when most listeners were suspicious of it in their own lives.
- She avoided the spotlight: skipped award ceremonies and was content to stay home as a mother, the opposite of Janis Joplin. Her line: you can get to know me through my music.
- Album cover: King at home by the windowsill, with her cat.

**Keys to *Tapestry*'s success**

- King's songwriting expertise and autobiography
- Sensual, R&B-influenced piano playing
- Warm, friendly vocals
- Lou Adler's uncluttered, "soft" production ("a very naked sounding album")
- Four Grammy awards
- An antidote to hard rock

**"It's Too Late" (1971)**

- Reflexive lyrics about the end of a relationship, from a feminist perspective; echoes her divorce from Goffin.
- Semi-autobiographical: lyrics co-written by Toni Stern.
- Critics stressed its "truth"; Robert Christgau called it a true breakup song.
- Minor key communicates sadness (a major key tends to sound brighter).
- Smooth production with no rough edges, unlike Taylor's bare acoustic guitar.
- Won the Grammy for Record of the Year (1972).

## Canadian singer-songwriters

The folk/singer-songwriter sound came to define "Canadian music" just as Canada's record industry was expanding, and arguably still does. The genre was seen as a retreat from politics, yet Canadian artists also used its personal voice for political stories.

### Canada's record industry in the 1970s

- **1971 Canadian content (CanCon) rules**: 30% of radio music must be by Canadian artists. Stations objected: little Canadian music had been pressed, and they wanted to play US hits.
- Record pressing plants built in the later 1970s helped the industry grow across the decade.
- Two career paths: stay in Canada (Gordon Lightfoot) or leave for the folk-rock scene around Los Angeles and its US industry resources (Joni Mitchell, Neil Young).
- Leonard Cohen and Buffy Sainte-Marie also found success outside Canada. Sainte-Marie's legacy was complicated by a 2023 exposé that her Indigenous ancestry could not be verified; she was stripped of several awards.
- Autobiography in Canadian songs often wove in history, nature, space and geography.

### Gordon Lightfoot

- From Orillia, Ontario; part of Toronto's Yorkville folk scene. Became an international success while staying in Canada.
- **"Canadian Railroad Trilogy" (1967)**, on *The Way I Feel*: commissioned by the CBC for Canada's centennial. A narrative about building the cross-country railway and its labourers; the tempo mirrors the work. Nation and sound become conflated.
- **"If You Could Read My Mind" (1970)**: reflexive, about his own marriage falling apart. Swelling chords and rhythmic arpeggio carry the emotion. His big international hit: a personal song travels better to US listeners than a story about Canadian trains.

### Joni Mitchell

- Born 1943, Fort Macleod, Alberta; Saskatoon, then California.
- **"Big Yellow Taxi" (1970)**, on *Ladies of the Canyon* (named for Laurel Canyon, LA).
- Environmental themes: nature lost to industrialization. Strummed chords instead of arpeggios suit an outward message meant to connect with others, not a private confession.

### Willie Dunn

- Mi'kmaq and Scottish/Irish heritage; born 1941, Montreal. Getting renewed attention through reissues such as *Native North America, Vol. 1*.
- **"I Pity the Country" (1971)**: colonialism and racism in Canada. Critical of the state, even as the genre was tied to state-backed cultural policy.
- **"The Ballad of Crowfoot" (1968)**: a 10-minute National Film Board (NFB) film made with an Indigenous filmmaking team; often called an early, even the first, music video.

## Funk (Week 9.2)

Funk comes into its own in the early 1970s: groove over melody, often a single chord, and a "fragmentation of soul." Many bands played their own blend: War; Tower of Power; Ohio Players; Earth, Wind & Fire; Parliament/Funkadelic.

### Why soul splinters into funk (Oct 28 lecture)

- **Fragmentation** is the theme of the early 1970s. Music becomes tied to identity, so the market splits into many genres, each with its own fans. Contrast: rock's cultural dominance across the 1960s.
- Soul tapers off at the end of the 1960s. The death of Otis Redding is one cited reason.
- The politics change shape. Civil-rights-era soul sang about change directly (direct action in the streets). Funk often turns to science fiction, space travel, new technology and imagined alternative futures (later called **Afrofuturism**).
- Funk is long and intentionally repetitive, less about melody and more about rhythm. It is built for dancing and socializing.
- Lineage: funk sets the stage for disco (next week), which spins off into hip hop and house.
- Not one sound: some bands stayed closer to soul (Earth, Wind & Fire); others borrowed from Jimi Hendrix.

### James Brown lays the groundwork

- Brown bridges soul and funk and stresses the first beat ("the one") over the backbeat.
- **"Cold Sweat" (1967)**: rhythmic groove, percussive instruments, and drums with a delayed fourth beat. Early rock 'n' roll put the backbeat on 2 and 4; the delayed beat de-centers it.
- Melody is de-emphasized: one clear chord change, at the bridge, in a song about 7 minutes long.
- **The break**: a moment where one instrument plays alone, here only the drums. A clean drum break can be looped, slowed down or sped up to build a disco or hip-hop beat (the "breakbeat"). Brown's breaks are among the most sampled ever.

### Jimi Hendrix supplies the experimental side

- **"Machine Gun" (1970, live)**: a 12-minute psychedelic piece where the guitar imitates the sounds of war and weapons, tied to the Vietnam War.
- Clinton works two paths at once: one rooted in James Brown's groove, one in Hendrix's innovations.

### George Clinton's "striking form of funk"

- A clear backbeat, often with electronic handclaps
- Thick texture with overlapping parts
- Bass lines by Bootsy Collins; synthesizer bass
- Gospel-rooted group vocals
- An elaborate stage show (spaceships descending onto the stage)

**P-Funk** is the umbrella term for the music and artists associated with Clinton. It later shaped West Coast **G-funk** hip hop.

|  | Funkadelic | Parliament |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Label | Warner Records | Casablanca |
| Style | Psychedelic, experimental funk (Hendrix side) | Shorter, simpler material (James Brown side) |
| Key album | *Maggot Brain* (1971) | *Mothership Connection* (1975) |

**"Maggot Brain" (1971)**

- Funk experimentalism: Hendrix-style guitar, studio mix
- Afrofuturist funk: spoken word, outer space
- Last album with the original line-up
- Influence of LSD and other drugs

**Mothership Connection (1975)**

- Parliament's stage show set "new standards for grandiosity in Black popular music."
- The album begins a "cosmological narrative on the redemptive power of funk."
- **"Give Up the Funk (Tear the Roof off the Sucker)"**: group vocals and a sing-along chorus; in the chorus the bass nearly doubles the vocal line; later a major influence on hip hop.

## Hard rock and heavy metal (Week 9.3)

Heavy metal (late 1960s to early 1970s) grows from the fragmentation of blues-infused rock: blues-based rock + psychedelic rock = its foundation. It is the antithesis of singer-songwriter "soft rock."

### Roots

- The London blues-rock scene and the improvisation of psychedelic rock (Jimi Hendrix)
- Riff-oriented songs as precursors: The Kinks, **"You Really Got Me" (1964)**; The Who, **"I Can't Explain" (1964/1965)**

### Key characteristics

- Extreme amplification
- Speed and volume: virtuosity, solos, 8th/16th notes
- Heavy bass and drums
- **Power chord**: the root and fifth of a chord, magnified by distortion
- A less reverent attitude toward the blues
- Darker themes and crude sexuality, versus the idealism of folk and psychedelia
- Audience: working-class and middle-class white youth, creating new class and age divisions among listeners

### Led Zeppelin

- *Rolling Stone* first called them "weak" and "unimaginative"; it found **"Babe I'm Gonna Leave You" (1969)** "very dull" and not worth its six and a half minutes.
- **"Whole Lotta Love"** (*Led Zeppelin II*, 1969): their first hit single. Heavy blues riff, sexuality expressed through the vocals, and an unstructured musical break with solos.
- It borrows from Muddy Waters' **"You Need Love" (1962)**, written by Willie Dixon. Remember this pair as an example of metal's less reverent use of the blues.

### Black Sabbath

- From Birmingham, England: a working-class area with a declining auto industry.
- Like Zeppelin: guitarist (Tony Iommi), bassist (Geezer Butler), drummer (Bill Ward) and a non-instrumentalist singer (Ozzy Osbourne); images of the occult and supernatural.
- "Songs stripped down to their riff-infused bones": *Black Sabbath* (1970) and *Paranoid* (1970 UK, 1971 US).
- Their darkness was "light years away" from Zeppelin's "flowery, hedonistic, and…romantic images"; tempos and textures that "ooze primordial sludge."
- **"Black Sabbath"** (*Black Sabbath*, 1970): the riff uses G, G an octave higher, and C-sharp. G to C-sharp is a **tritone**, called "the devil in music" (*diabolus in musica*) in the Middle Ages. Riff and tempo hold for most of the song.
- **"Paranoid"** (*Paranoid*, their second album): their first single, built around Iommi's guitar riff; influenced both metal and punk.

### What the critics said

- **Greil Marcus**: the simplified sound succeeded because a younger audience had not absorbed the aesthetic premises of 1960s rock.
- **Lester Bangs**: "since when is monotony so taboo in rock and roll, anyway?"

| Image | Flowery, hedonistic, romantic | Dark, occult, supernatural |
| Sound | Blues riffs + long improvised breaks | Stripped-down riffs, slow "sludge" |
| Key song | "Whole Lotta Love" (1969) | "Black Sabbath", "Paranoid" (1970) |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fragmentation | One dominant style (1960s rock) splits into many genres with separate audiences | Theme of the whole week |
| Singer-songwriter | Performer who writes intimate, introspective, confessional songs from semi-autobiographical themes | Carole King, James Taylor, Joni Mitchell |
| "The personal is political" | Feminist maxim: private experience carries political meaning | Women singer-songwriters, "It's Too Late" |
| Reflexivity | Lyrics that look back on and examine the singer's own experience | "It's Too Late", "If You Could Read My Mind" |
| Rhythmic arpeggio | Notes of a chord played one after another, not together | Singer-songwriter accompaniment |
| Fingerpicking | Plucking strings with individual fingers to play arpeggio patterns | James Taylor, "You've Got a Friend" |
| Riff | Short repeated musical figure a song is built on | Heavy metal (singer-songwriters avoid it) |
| Backbeat | Accent on beats 2 and 4 | Early rock 'n' roll; Clinton's handclaps |
| "The one" | Strong stress on the first beat of the bar | James Brown |
| Break / breakbeat | Passage where one instrument (often drums) plays alone; easy to loop and sample | "Cold Sweat"; later disco and hip hop |
| Afrofuturism | Funk imagery of outer space, technology and alternative futures | *Maggot Brain*, *Mothership Connection* |
| P-Funk | Umbrella term for George Clinton's music and artists | Parliament, Funkadelic |
| G-funk | West Coast hip-hop style shaped by P-Funk | Hip hop (later unit) |
| Power chord | Root and fifth of a chord, magnified by distortion | Heavy metal |
| Tritone | Interval of three whole tones (e.g. G to C-sharp); *diabolus in musica*, "the devil in music" | "Black Sabbath" |
